# A possible next test: unfinished connections versus reconnecting earlier words

**Context added after the round-3 plan was frozen, on 23 September 2026.** This is a short, descriptive literature check and a proposed future test. It was written without viewing the final test data. It changes no current equation, target, prediction, or analysis. No new experiment was run.

There are already mathematical accounts of memory demands during sentence comprehension. They suggest more specific measurements to try; they do not supply a proven formula for this app’s score.

## Three original research papers

| Paper | Useful idea and evidence | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| **Gibson (1998), “Linguistic complexity: Locality of syntactic dependencies,” Cognition 68, 1–76.** [Author-hosted paper](https://tedlab.mit.edu/tedlab_website/researchpapers/Gibson_1998_Cogn.pdf). DOI: 10.1016/S0010-0277(98)00034-1. | Separates keeping unfinished grammatical requirements in memory from connecting a new word to earlier material. It specifies numerical cost functions and confronts them with sentence-processing contrasts. See §§2.2–2.4 and §6. | The paper itself considers alternative cost functions. Its abstract “memory units” are theoretical quantities, not calibrated measurements of a student’s memory or app-score units. |
| **Chen, Gibson & Wolf (2005), “Online syntactic storage costs in sentence comprehension,” Journal of Memory and Language 52, 144–169.** [Author-hosted paper](https://tedlab.mit.edu/tedlab_website/researchpapers/Chen_et_al_2005_JML.pdf). DOI: 10.1016/j.jml.2004.10.001. | Three human reading experiments tested unfinished grammatical expectations. In Experiment 1, the critical region was read more slowly when more verbs were still required. Experiments 2–3 tested another unfinished connection. See pp.147–151 and General Discussion, pp.160–163. | This supports particular human processing predictions. It does not validate a universal count of every unfinished dependency; the discussion explicitly distinguishes competing definitions and cross-language problems. |
| **Grodner & Gibson (2005), “Consequences of the Serial Nature of Linguistic Input for Sentential Complexity,” Cognitive Science 29.** [Author-hosted paper](https://tedlab.mit.edu/tedlab_website/researchpapers/Grodner_%26_Gibson_2005_CogSci.pdf). DOI: 10.1207/s15516709cog0000_7. | Two human reading studies tested the cost of connecting words separated by intervening material. The proposed integration cost increases with distance. See §1.2, Experiments 1–2, and General Discussion. | The studied contrasts did not separate all possible distance measures. The discussion also addresses cases where extra material helps readers anticipate a word, rather than slowing them down. “Farther always means harder” would overstate the evidence. |

These are **human-tested processing hypotheses**, not validated instruments for measuring overall cognitive load. Reading time is an observed behavior; the proposed memory mechanism is an explanation to test. None of these studies tests Jev or the Working-Memory-Jev score. The papers share authors and a theoretical tradition; this is not an independent-replication survey or a systematic literature review. Full PDFs were accessible; the definitions, relevant experiment descriptions and discussions above were inspected. No participant-level data were reanalyzed.

## One proposed experiment, within approach 1

**Question:** Does explicitly tracking unfinished grammatical requirements and intervening new information improve the existing direct equation’s app-score predictions?

The current measurements already include mean and maximum dependency distance, long-dependency counts, and summed spans (`equation-research/features.py`). Adding ordinary word distance again would not be a meaningful new test.

1. Add just two predefined inputs: the highest number of unfinished requirements during a reading step, and a count of intervening new entity mentions and finite verbs at connection completions. The latter distinguishes new information from ordinary word distance. Define entity novelty, connection types and both counts with written rules using the permitted local parser and only the available prefix. A small, score-blinded hand check should establish whether the rules actually measure the intended structures. This would be a simplified implementation inspired by the papers, not an exact reproduction of their theories.
2. Fit two versions on the same development families with identical fitting settings: the existing measurements, and those measurements plus the two new inputs. Fix both versions before obtaining new test answers. The same equation applies to every passage.
3. Test on new, matched families that vary unfinished requirements and intervening new information while matching vocabulary, length and plausibility as closely as possible. Include pairs with the same word distance but different repeated-versus-new entities. Keep all variants together. Also record whether the app’s scores respond to these manipulations; human reading findings cannot substitute for that check.
4. Declare the proposed primary claim in advance: the added inputs reduce equal-family average absolute error by at least **0.10 app-score units**. A paired family interval entirely below 0.10 would contradict that improvement claim; one entirely above 0.10 would provisionally support it; an interval crossing 0.10 would leave the magnitude unresolved. Set the sample size and interval procedure before collecting answers.

If the app does not respond to the controlled differences, that challenges this route to copying its score—not the human findings. If the app responds but the revised equation misses the differences, that challenges the implementation or fitted mapping. Neither outcome proves that every possible equation is impossible. Human validation would remain a separate study.
