These equations try to reproduce the app’s score at each reading step. They use the existing local text parser and word statistics, followed by saved numerical rules. Once installed, they make no API calls. There is no restriction on equation length: every saved coefficient, comparison, and decision rule is part of the calculation.

The parser first divides the complete passage into steps. The equations were developed against the app’s fixed grade-six, visible-text learner profile. They do not measure an individual student’s knowledge or memory.

**1. Direct equation: compare measurements with stored examples.** It measures properties of the current step and the text preceding it: word counts, grammatical relationships, references, word familiarity, and other text features. It puts measurements on comparable scales, then calculates how similar they are to stored measurements from the training examples. Each similarity contributes a learned amount, which can add to or subtract from a starting value. The current export rounds the result after preventing negative scores. In words: **starting value + weighted contributions from similar measured examples**. Its parameters are fixed when it runs.

**A variation of approach 1: estimate the chances of each possible score.** Weighted sums of text measurements are converted into estimated score probabilities. The current version learns probabilities for an individual reference score, then uses arithmetic to derive probabilities for the middle score from three independent draws. It reports the predicted median of that distribution; its unrounded output is the probability-weighted mean. No new Jev calls or random draws occur during use. The conversion assumes the three draws share the modeled distribution.

**2. Smaller-decision equation: estimate what counts separately, then add.** The parser supplies possible ideas in the current step and a limited selection of earlier ideas. Many saved decision trees ask questions about their text and grammar measurements. Their combined answers estimate three chances for each candidate: it counts separately, belongs with another idea, or is background. A second collection of trees estimates additional relationship units. The current equation adds each candidate’s estimated chance of counting separately, adds the estimated extra units, and rounds once. These are probabilities about intermediate decisions. Adding their averages and rounding is an approximation to the final reference score; it does not calculate the complete probability distribution of the median of three Jev scores.

**3. Directed ledger: track identities and relationships through the prefix.** This method builds a running record of entities and who does what to whom. Explicit rules match repeated references, attempt simple pronoun resolution, recognize some definitions, distinguish reversed roles and negation, and retire certain old location facts. A recent-history window helps determine what remains active. It then measures the record: new or reused entities, active relations, retrieved conditions, and similar quantities. The current exported equation adds contributions from decision trees using those measurements, then rounds. The tracking rules can make mistakes; the final score is learned rather than simply the number of entries in the record.

Matching these app outputs and measuring human working-memory demand remain separate research questions.


The fixed combination chosen for smaller development error is **round(0.4 × direct + 0.5 × components + 0.1 × ledger)**. Each name means that method’s unrounded output; the weights are the same for every passage. The result is prevented from going below zero. Here, round means the nearest whole number, with exact halves rounded upward: 2.5 becomes 3. The separate exact-match combination and every precise parameter are preserved in offline/models.json and offline/models/.
